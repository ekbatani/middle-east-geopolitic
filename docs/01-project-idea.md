# Middle East Geopolitical Intelligence Platform

## 1. Document purpose

This document defines the product idea, analytical scope, operating principles, users, capabilities, information model, and success criteria for a Middle East geopolitical intelligence platform.

The platform is intended to collect, structure, verify, analyze, and report information about Middle Eastern countries, wars, political relationships, non-state actors, economic pressure, energy security, and external-power involvement. It combines a Python intelligence backend with REST APIs, scheduled reporting, investigations, and alerts.

This document describes **what the system is and why it should exist**. Technical implementation details are defined separately in `02-implementation-design.md`.

---

## 2. Product vision

Build a continuously updated, evidence-backed geopolitical intelligence system that helps an analyst understand:

- the strategic position of each Middle Eastern country;
- relations among states, governments, armed forces, organizations, and external powers;
- active conflicts, proxy networks, and escalation pathways;
- changes in political stability, military posture, economic resilience, and public pressure;
- how events affect regional alliances, energy markets, trade routes, and humanitarian conditions;
- which claims are verified, disputed, unsupported, or still under investigation;
- which indicators suggest escalation, de-escalation, regime instability, maritime disruption, or wider war;
- how current events change predefined scenarios and forecasts.

The system must behave as an intelligence-analysis workspace, not as a generic news chatbot.

---

## 3. Core problem

Middle East geopolitical analysis is difficult because relevant information is:

- distributed across many languages and jurisdictions;
- published by actors with competing interests;
- often duplicated, delayed, incomplete, or deliberately misleading;
- highly time-sensitive;
- expressed through military, diplomatic, economic, religious, tribal, cyber, and informational channels;
- affected by both state and non-state actors;
- difficult to compare consistently across countries and conflicts;
- prone to false certainty when generated by large language models without structured evidence.

A normal search or chat workflow produces fragmented answers. It does not preserve source provenance, historical changes, unresolved contradictions, risk-score changes, or prediction accuracy.

The platform solves this by maintaining an authoritative, structured, time-aware intelligence model.

## 4. Product principles

### 4.1 Evidence before conclusion

Every important assessment must be traceable to evidence. The system must preserve the original source, extracted text, publication date, retrieval date, source identity, and the exact claim supported or contradicted by that source.

### 4.2 Claims are not facts

A statement by a government, military, armed group, witness, media outlet, or anonymous official is stored as a claim. It becomes an assessed or approved fact only after verification rules are satisfied.

### 4.3 Time is part of every record

Countries, governments, alliances, conflicts, leadership structures, and relationships change. The model must preserve historical states instead of overwriting old values.

### 4.4 Actors are not interchangeable

The platform must distinguish among:

- a country and its current government;
- a government and its armed forces;
- a population and its political leadership;
- a state sponsor and an aligned non-state actor;
- material dependence, political alignment, and operational control;
- rhetoric, intent, capability, preparation, and action.

### 4.5 Uncertainty must be visible

Every assessment must include a confidence level, information gaps, and disputed points. Probability ranges are preferred over false precision.

### 4.6 Human control over high-impact conclusions

High-impact events and assessments require analyst approval before they are used as canonical facts in official reports.

### 4.7 Explainable scoring

Risk scores must be based on explicit indicators. The system must show why a score changed, which evidence contributed, and whether an LLM adjustment was applied.

### 4.8 Source diversity

For disputed matters, the platform must compare sources from multiple perspectives and avoid relying on one political or geographic information ecosystem.

### 4.9 Security by design

External pages, documents, and messages are untrusted data. Instructions embedded in collected content must never be treated as agent instructions.

## 9. Active strategic systems

The platform should organize regional developments into strategic systems rather than isolated headlines.

### 9.1 Iran-Israel-United States escalation system

Monitor direct and indirect attacks, nuclear facilities, missile and drone activity, U.S. regional bases, Gulf infrastructure, proxy mobilization, diplomatic channels, declared red lines, and changes in target categories.

### 9.2 Israel-Palestine conflict system

Monitor Gaza, the West Bank, hostages and prisoners, governance, Israeli domestic politics, Palestinian political legitimacy, settlements, humanitarian conditions, Arab normalization, and international diplomatic or legal pressure.

### 9.3 Gulf security and strategic balancing

Monitor differing policies of Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Oman, Kuwait, and Bahrain toward the United States, Iran, China, Russia, Israel, energy security, mediation, and defence cooperation.

### 9.4 Lebanon-Israel-Hezbollah system

Monitor border activity, force posture, Lebanese state authority, internal political conditions, reconstruction, external support, and escalation thresholds.

### 9.5 Syria territorial and political order

Monitor government control, Kurdish-led areas, Turkish presence, Israeli operations, Iranian and Russian influence, armed-group integration, sanctions, refugee return, and reconstruction.

### 9.6 Iraq strategic competition system

Monitor federal authority, armed factions, U.S. presence, Iranian influence, Kurdish politics, Turkish operations, oil exports, Shia political competition, and service-delivery pressure.

### 9.7 Yemen and Red Sea security

Monitor Houthi activity, Saudi-Houthi negotiations, internal fragmentation, UAE-backed groups, maritime attacks, Bab el-Mandeb, ports, missiles, drones, and humanitarian conditions.

### 9.8 Türkiye multi-theatre strategy

Monitor Syria, Iraq, Kurdish conflict, NATO, Russia, Iran, Israel, the eastern Mediterranean, the Caucasus, energy corridors, defence exports, and domestic political-economic pressure.

### 9.9 Nuclear and missile proliferation

Monitor enrichment, inspections, weaponization indicators, missile ranges, warhead capacity, dual-use technology, regional missile defence, and pre-emptive-strike doctrines.

### 9.10 Economic and transport corridors

Monitor Belt and Road projects, the International North-South Transport Corridor, India-Middle East-Europe Corridor, Iraq Development Road, Gulf ports, Caucasus corridors, pipelines, railways, and electricity interconnections.

## 10. Core information objects

### 10.1 Source

A source is an original publication, data feed, document, media item, statement, image, or dataset.

### 10.2 Document

A document is the archived and normalized representation of a source item.

### 10.3 Claim

A claim is a specific assertion made by an identifiable source or actor.

### 10.4 Evidence

Evidence links a source or document passage to a claim and indicates whether it supports, contradicts, partially supports, contextualizes, or merely repeats the claim.

### 10.5 Event

An event is a time-bounded occurrence involving one or more actors, locations, actions, and consequences.

### 10.6 Relationship

A relationship represents a durable connection between two actors, such as cooperation, rivalry, dependence, sponsorship, conflict, or diplomatic engagement.

### 10.7 Relationship observation

A time-stamped assessment of the current state of a relationship across multiple dimensions.

### 10.8 Indicator

A measurable or assessable signal used to calculate risk or evaluate scenarios.

### 10.9 Risk assessment

A time-stamped score and explanation for a defined risk category.

### 10.10 Scenario

A structured possible future containing assumptions, triggers, leading indicators, consequences, probability range, confidence, and invalidation criteria.

### 10.11 Investigation

A managed analytical workflow created to answer a specific question or resolve a disputed event.

### 10.12 Report

A generated analytical product based on approved or clearly labeled assessed data.

### 10.13 Forecast record

A stored prediction with issue date, target date, probability, evidence, assumptions, and later outcome evaluation.

## 11. Intelligence lifecycle

Every collected item moves through explicit states.

### 11.1 Observed

Raw material has been collected and archived.

### 11.2 Extracted

The system has identified candidate actors, claims, events, locations, and dates.

### 11.3 Assessed

Evidence has been compared and confidence assigned.

### 11.4 Approved

An analyst has approved the item for canonical use.

### 11.5 Superseded

A newer assessment replaces the previous interpretation while preserving history.

### 11.6 Rejected

The item was determined to be false, irrelevant, duplicated, or unusable. Rejection reasons are stored.

---

## 12. Verification model

Verification is not a binary source-reputation decision.

For each claim, the system should evaluate:

- source identity;
- directness of knowledge;
- independence from other reports;
- corroboration;
- contradiction;
- temporal proximity;
- geographic proximity;
- availability of imagery, documents, or data;
- source incentives;
- known corrections or retractions;
- consistency with observable capabilities;
- confidence and unresolved questions.

Suggested statuses:

- unreviewed;
- unsupported;
- single-source claim;
- partially corroborated;
- verified;
- disputed;
- contradicted;
- false;
- unverifiable.

## 13. Risk model

The platform must maintain separate risk categories rather than one generalized geopolitical score.

Initial risk categories:

- interstate war;
- civil conflict;
- government instability;
- proxy escalation;
- maritime disruption;
- energy disruption;
- humanitarian deterioration;
- nuclear escalation;
- cyber escalation;
- diplomatic breakdown;
- sanctions escalation;
- critical-infrastructure disruption.

Every assessment includes:

- current score from 0 to 100;
- previous score;
- trend;
- score contribution by indicator;
- explanation;
- supporting evidence;
- counter-indicators;
- confidence;
- last-updated timestamp;
- model and ruleset version;
- analyst approval status.

---

## 14. Scenario model

Each major crisis should maintain at least four scenario families:

1. controlled de-escalation;
2. managed confrontation;
3. regional escalation;
4. systemic regional war.

Each scenario includes:

- scope and time horizon;
- initial conditions;
- assumptions;
- trigger events;
- leading indicators;
- actor behavior;
- military consequences;
- diplomatic consequences;
- economic and energy consequences;
- humanitarian consequences;
- beneficiaries and disadvantaged actors;
- probability range;
- confidence;
- evidence;
- invalidation criteria;
- changes from the previous assessment.

The system must never silently update scenario probability. Every change requires an explanation.

## 15. Operational interfaces

The API and application services form the operational layer.

The operational interfaces should:

- answer intelligence queries using the Python intelligence API;
- retrieve current profiles, events, claims, relationships, risks, and scenarios;
- launch investigations;
- request reports;
- create and manage user-facing monitoring jobs;
- send approved alerts through connected channels;
- explain uncertainty and score changes;
- guide analysts through evidence review.

Operational interfaces must not:

- access the production database directly without authorization;
- approve high-impact facts autonomously;
- publish reports without the required authorization;
- execute unrestricted commands;
- follow instructions embedded in collected content.

## 16. User-facing workflows

### 16.1 Daily situation brief

The user asks for or receives a daily report containing:

- executive assessment;
- material changes in the last 24 hours;
- country and relationship changes;
- active conflict updates;
- military posture changes;
- diplomatic developments;
- economic, energy, and maritime developments;
- humanitarian developments;
- risk-score changes;
- scenario changes;
- unresolved claims;
- indicators to monitor next.

### 16.2 Event investigation

The user submits a report, URL, or question. The system:

1. identifies the event and claims;
2. searches for independent evidence;
3. compares narratives;
4. checks duplicates and historical context;
5. assesses confidence;
6. identifies unresolved questions;
7. proposes an event record;
8. requests approval when required.

### 16.3 Country profile

The user requests a current country assessment. The system returns political, military, economic, diplomatic, social, energy, and internal-security dimensions with evidence and trend history.

### 16.4 Relationship comparison

The user compares two or more bilateral relationships across standardized dimensions.

### 16.5 Risk-change explanation

The user asks why a score changed. The system presents indicator contributions, new evidence, counter-indicators, LLM adjustment if used, confidence, and approval history.

### 16.6 Scenario simulation

The user introduces a hypothetical event. The system creates a non-canonical simulation that estimates affected actors, relationships, risks, and scenarios without modifying production intelligence.

### 16.7 Monitoring subscription

The user creates a monitor such as:

- notify me if the Strait of Hormuz disruption score exceeds 70;
- alert me when verified direct attacks occur between specified actors;
- send a weekly Türkiye regional strategy report;
- report meaningful changes in Iran-Saudi relations.

## 21. Ethical and safety boundaries

The platform is designed for analysis, reporting, risk assessment, and public-source research.

It must not be designed to:

- select or optimize military targets;
- provide operational attack planning;
- facilitate violent wrongdoing;
- expose protected personal data;
- conduct unauthorized surveillance;
- impersonate official actors;
- automatically amplify propaganda;
- publish unverified casualty or attribution claims as fact.

Sensitive outputs should be access-controlled and logged.

## 22. MVP scope

The first useful release should:

1. model 20 to 30 major state and non-state actors;
2. collect from 50 curated sources;
3. archive and normalize source documents;
4. extract candidate actors, claims, and events;
5. deduplicate similar reports;
6. support analyst assessment and approval;
7. maintain approximately 20 explicit risk indicators;
8. track multidimensional actor relationships;
9. generate a daily regional brief;
10. answer intelligence queries through controlled API endpoints;
11. launch event investigations as background jobs;
12. deliver approved alerts through one messaging channel;
13. preserve evidence and confidence for every conclusion;
14. audit predictions and scenario changes.

The MVP should not include unrestricted autonomous browsing, automated publishing, Kubernetes, multiple independent agents, or black-box geopolitical prediction.

## 23. Product success criteria

### 23.1 Data quality

- At least 95% of approved events include traceable evidence.
- Duplicate event rate remains below 5% after review.
- Every approved risk-score change has an explanation and evidence bundle.
- High-impact events cannot become canonical without required approval.

### 23.2 Operational quality

- Daily brief generation completes reliably.
- Source collection failures are visible and retried.
- Intelligence queries return current facts directly from database state.
- Investigation state is durable and resumable.

### 23.3 Analytical quality

- Claims and facts are consistently separated.
- Contradictory evidence is visible.
- Scenario probability changes are explained.
- Forecast calibration improves over time.
- Analysts can reproduce the reasoning behind reports.

### 23.4 User value

- The user can understand meaningful regional changes without reviewing every source.
- The user can trace a conclusion to evidence in a small number of steps.
- The user can create monitors and receive only material alerts.
- The user can compare countries and relationships using consistent dimensions.

## 25. Final product definition

The Middle East Geopolitical Intelligence Platform is a source-driven, structured, auditable system in which:

- Python owns collection, data, workflows, scoring, analysis, and reporting;
- PostgreSQL stores authoritative and historical intelligence records;
- background workers perform durable collection and analytical jobs;
- object storage preserves original documents and generated artifacts;
- language models assist with extraction, comparison, explanation, and scenario analysis;
- deterministic rules and human approval provide control;
- structured APIs and interfaces provide monitoring, investigations, reports, and alerts.

The result should be a real intelligence-analysis platform, not a chatbot that summarizes geopolitical news.
